# Appendix A-3: Market Greenlight Scorecard Template

> The scoring tool the VP of Expansion uses to evaluate, compare, and approve new markets for launch.

## Purpose

Not every market that looks promising on a map survives contact with reality. This scorecard exists to force structured, repeatable evaluation of every candidate market before Jetson commits capital, people, and executive attention to a launch. It replaces gut feel with weighted scoring across five categories that predict whether a market will actually work — not just whether it sounds good in a strategy deck.

The VP of Expansion fills this out (or delegates it to the Market Launch Lead for initial research), reviews it with cross-functional leads, and uses the weighted score to make a greenlight, conditional, or no-go decision. Every market that Jetson enters should have a completed scorecard on file.

> **Core principle:** A disciplined scorecard doesn't slow you down — it prevents you from spending six months and $500K learning what a two-week analysis would have told you. Score first. Commit second.

## 1) Scoring Categories & Weights

The scorecard evaluates five categories. Each category carries a weight reflecting its relative importance to launch success. All scores use a 1–5 scale.

## 2) Category 1 — Demand Signal (25%)

This is the "is there a market here" check. A high score means the market has a large addressable base, clear conversion opportunity, and customers already looking for what Jetson offers.

**Scoring Factors:**

- Total addressable homes with ducted HVAC systems
- Gas-to-electric conversion opportunity (% of homes currently on gas)
- Inbound lead volume and/or website traffic originating from the market
- Climate suitability for heat pumps (heating degree days, cooling degree days, extreme temp frequency)

**1–5 Scale Definitions:**

| Score | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **5** | Large addressable base (500K+ ducted homes), high gas penetration (70%+), measurable inbound demand already exists, climate is ideal for heat pump performance year-round. |
| **4** | Strong base (250K–500K ducted homes), solid gas penetration (50–70%), some organic inbound signals, climate favorable with minor seasonal limitations. |
| **3** | Moderate base (100K–250K ducted homes), moderate gas penetration (30–50%), limited inbound signals, climate workable but requires cold-climate or dual-fuel solutions for part of the year. |
| **2** | Small base (50K–100K ducted homes), low gas penetration (15–30%), negligible inbound demand, climate challenges require significant product/process adaptation. |
| **1** | Minimal base (<50K ducted homes), very low gas penetration (<15%), no measurable demand signal, climate is hostile to heat pump economics without heavy supplemental heating. |

> **Data sources:** Census/ACS housing data, utility customer counts, HubSpot lead geo-reports, Google Trends, ENERGY STAR climate zone maps. The Market Launch Lead compiles; the VP of Expansion validates.

---

## 3) Category 2 — Regulatory & Incentive Environment (25%)

Rebates and incentives drive customer economics. Licensing and permitting complexity drive Jetson's speed-to-market and ongoing operational cost. This category is weighted equally with demand because a great market with no incentives and hostile permitting can kill unit economics.

**Scoring Factors:**

- State and/or utility rebate programs (dollar amount per install, ease of enrollment, reliability of payment)
- Licensing requirements complexity (state HVAC/electrical license, reciprocity, exam difficulty, timeline)
- Permitting requirements complexity (number of municipalities, variation in requirements, inspection timelines)
- Building code favorability (codes that encourage or mandate electrification vs. codes that are neutral or restrictive)

**1–5 Scale Definitions:**

| Score | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **5** | Strong rebates ($3K+ per install available to most customers), simple enrollment, fast payment. Single state license with straightforward process. Permitting is centralized or highly standardized. Building codes actively favor electrification. |
| **4** | Good rebates ($1.5K–$3K), reasonable enrollment, some delays possible. Licensing is manageable within 60 days. Permitting varies somewhat by municipality but is navigable. Codes are neutral to favorable. |
| **3** | Moderate rebates ($500–$1.5K) or rebates available only to a subset of customers. Licensing requires 90+ days or has reciprocity limitations. Permitting varies significantly across the service area. Codes are neutral. |
| **2** | Weak rebates (<$500) or only available through difficult-to-access programs. Licensing is complex (multiple exams, long timelines, or apprenticeship requirements). Permitting is fragmented and unpredictable. Some code barriers exist. |
| **1** | No meaningful rebates available. Licensing requires 1+ year apprenticeship or equivalent barrier. Permitting is hostile (long delays, excessive inspections, high fees). Building codes actively discourage or restrict heat pump installations. |

---

## 4) Category 3 — Competitive Landscape (15%)

Jetson doesn't need to be the only player in a market, but it does need room to compete. This category assesses how crowded the market is and whether Jetson can differentiate.

**Scoring Factors:**

- Number and strength of existing heat pump installers (national players, strong local brands, or fragmented small shops)
- Pricing environment (race-to-bottom vs. room for premium positioning)
- Brand awareness opportunity (is the market underserved, or is every homeowner already getting mailers from three competitors)

**1–5 Scale Definitions:**

| Score | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **5** | Few or no dedicated heat pump installers. Market is dominated by traditional HVAC shops that treat heat pumps as a side offering. Clear white space for a premium, electrification-focused brand. |
| **4** | Some competitors exist but none have strong brand recognition or operational scale. Pricing supports healthy margins. Room to establish Jetson as the category leader. |
| **3** | Moderate competition. One or two established players plus several smaller shops. Pricing is competitive but not destructive. Jetson can compete on quality and experience but won't be the only option customers consider. |
| **2** | Crowded market with multiple well-funded competitors. Price pressure is real. Winning market share requires significant marketing spend and/or aggressive pricing. |
| **1** | Market is saturated. National players and strong local brands have locked up utility partnerships, contractor networks, and customer mindshare. Breaking in would require disproportionate investment relative to the opportunity. |

---

## 5) Category 4 — Operational Feasibility (20%)

Can Jetson actually build and run a market here? Demand and economics don't matter if you can't hire technicians, find a warehouse, or get trucks to job sites efficiently.

**Scoring Factors:**

- Labor market availability (HVAC technicians, electricians, apprentice pipeline)
- Warehouse/facility availability and cost (suitable spaces in the service area at reasonable lease rates)
- Proximity to existing Jetson markets (for training rotations, support deployments, and management oversight)
- Fleet/logistics feasibility (service area geography, drive times, charging infrastructure for EV fleet)

**1–5 Scale Definitions:**

| Score | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **5** | Strong HVAC/electrical labor pool. Multiple suitable warehouse options available. Adjacent to or within 2 hours of an existing Jetson market. Service area is compact with manageable drive times. |
| **4** | Adequate labor pool with some recruiting effort required. Warehouse options exist at reasonable cost. Within a half-day travel of an existing market. Service area is workable. |
| **3** | Labor market is tight — recruiting will require trade school partnerships or relocation packages. Limited warehouse options or above-average lease costs. No adjacent Jetson market but air travel is straightforward. Service area is large but manageable with route optimization. |
| **2** | Labor shortage is a real constraint — hiring the first crew will take 8+ weeks and may require importing talent. Warehouse options are scarce or expensive. No nearby Jetson presence; market is operationally isolated. Service area sprawl creates logistics challenges. |
| **1** | Severe labor shortage with no viable pipeline. No suitable facilities available. Market is geographically isolated from any Jetson presence. Service area logistics are impractical (extreme distances, poor road infrastructure, no EV charging network). |

---

## 6) Category 5 — Financial Viability (15%)

Does the math work? This category tests whether the market can support a profitable Jetson operation at scale — not just whether there's demand, but whether the cost structure allows Jetson to make money.

**Scoring Factors:**

- Projected cost per install vs. revenue (labor rates, material costs, travel costs, rebate processing costs relative to average selling price)
- Time to breakeven for the market branch (months from soft launch to contribution-positive)
- Market size required for minimum viable branch (can the market sustain the volume needed to cover fixed costs)

**1–5 Scale Definitions:**

| Score | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **5** | Unit economics are strong — projected gross margin exceeds company average. Breakeven within 6 months of go-live [TBD — confirm with Brett]. Market can sustain 2x+ the minimum viable branch volume. |
| **4** | Unit economics are healthy — projected gross margin is at or near company average. Breakeven within 9 months [TBD]. Market comfortably supports minimum viable volume. |
| **3** | Unit economics are workable but tight. Some cost headwinds (higher labor rates, material transport costs, etc.). Breakeven within 12 months [TBD]. Market supports minimum viable volume with limited margin for error. |
| **2** | Unit economics are challenged. Cost per install is above company average due to labor, logistics, or regulatory costs. Breakeven projected at 15+ months [TBD]. Market may not support minimum viable volume without significant demand generation investment. |
| **1** | Unit economics do not work under current assumptions. Cost per install exceeds revenue potential without significant pricing or incentive changes. Breakeven is 18+ months or uncertain [TBD]. Market cannot support minimum viable branch. |

## 8) Greenlight Thresholds

The total weighted score determines the market's status. These thresholds are the decision framework — not a suggestion.

**Thresholds [TBD — confirm with Brett]:**

| Total Weighted Score | Decision | What Happens Next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **>= 3.5** | **Greenlight** | Market is approved for launch. Assign a Market Launch Lead and begin the T-minus countdown (Section 4.1). VP of Expansion confirms and communicates the decision. |
| **2.5 – 3.4** | **Conditional** | Market has potential but carries material risks. A mitigation plan must be developed for every category scoring below 3.0. VP of Expansion reviews the mitigation plan and makes a go/no-go call within 2 weeks [TBD]. |
| **< 2.5** | **No-Go** | Market does not meet the bar for launch under current conditions. File the scorecard for future reference — conditions change. Revisit in 6–12 months or when a material factor shifts (e.g., new rebate program launches, competitor exits). |

> **On "Conditional" markets:** Conditional does not mean "greenlight with an asterisk." It means the market has identified risks that need a concrete plan before capital and people are committed. The mitigation plan must address: what specifically is the risk, what action mitigates it, who owns it, and by when. If the mitigation plan isn't credible, the market is a no-go.

## 9) Kill Criteria — Automatic Disqualifiers

Some conditions make a market a non-starter regardless of the overall weighted score. If any of the following are true, the market is an automatic no-go. Do not waste time completing the full scorecard.

**Kill Criteria [TBD — confirm with Brett]:**

| Kill Criterion | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| **State law prohibits or severely restricts heat pump installations** | No legal path to operate. Full stop. |
| **No available rebate or incentive programs** (state, federal passthrough, or utility) | Without incentives, customer economics collapse. Jetson's model depends on rebates closing the affordability gap. A market with zero incentive support is not viable until that changes. |
| **Licensing requires 2+ year apprenticeship** or equivalent barrier with no reciprocity pathway | Time-to-market is too long. Jetson cannot wait 2+ years to get licensed in a state with no reciprocity for existing credentials. Revisit if reciprocity legislation passes. |
| **No viable labor pipeline** (metro area has fewer than [TBD] licensed HVAC techs per 100K population and no trade school within 50 miles) | You can't staff a market that doesn't have tradespeople. If the pipeline doesn't exist and there's no mechanism to build one in a reasonable timeframe, the market isn't ready. |
| **Service area geography makes logistics impractical** (average drive time to job sites exceeds [TBD] minutes from any feasible warehouse location) | Labor efficiency dies when technicians spend half their day driving. If the market's geography doesn't support a reasonable service radius, the unit economics won't work. |

> **Note:** Kill criteria are binary. They are not scores to be weighed — they are gates. If a kill criterion is triggered, the market is a no-go until the underlying condition changes. The VP of Expansion can override a kill criterion in extraordinary circumstances, but the override and its rationale must be documented.

---

## 10) How to Use This Scorecard

**Step 1: Check Kill Criteria First.** Before spending any time on research and scoring, run through the kill criteria. If any trigger, stop. File it and move on.

**Step 2: Research & Score Each Category.** The Market Launch Lead (or whoever is assigned the initial analysis) researches each of the five categories using the data sources and scoring definitions above. Document your sources and assumptions — the VP of Expansion will challenge them.

**Step 3: Calculate the Weighted Score.** Multiply each raw score by its category weight. Sum the weighted scores for the total.

**Step 4: Present to VP of Expansion.** Bring the completed scorecard, supporting data, and (if the score is in the conditional range) a draft mitigation plan. Be prepared to defend your scores.

**Step 5: Decision & Documentation.** The VP of Expansion makes the greenlight, conditional, or no-go call. The completed scorecard — including the decision and any conditions — is filed in the market's launch folder in Notion.

**Step 6: Revisit as Conditions Change.** Markets that are no-go today may become viable as rebate programs launch, competitors exit, or labor markets shift. Scorecards are living documents — update and re-present when material conditions change.

---

## If You Only Remember 3 Things

1. **Score first, commit second.** Every market gets a scorecard before Jetson commits capital, people, or executive attention. No exceptions. The scorecard exists to prevent expensive lessons that a two-week analysis would have caught.
2. **Kill criteria are gates, not guidelines.** If a kill criterion triggers, the market is a no-go — full stop. Don't rationalize your way past a structural barrier. Wait for the condition to change.
3. **Conditional is not a soft yes.** A score of 2.5–3.4 means the market has real risks that need a real mitigation plan. If you can't write a credible plan that addresses every sub-3.0 category, it's a no-go wearing a conditional hat.

# Appendix A-8: Lessons Learned Template

> The standardized framework for capturing what worked, what didn't, and what to change — so every market launch makes the next one better.

## Purpose

Every market launch must produce a lessons learned document. No exceptions. This is not bureaucracy — this is how the playbook gets better.

This template standardizes how Jetson captures and shares what worked, what didn't, and what to change for the next launch. The Massachusetts expansion was Jetson's first major lesson. That experience proved that the cost of not documenting is paid by the next Market Launch Lead who walks into the same avoidable problems. This template ensures we never stop learning.

The lessons learned document is a required deliverable — just like the stabilization scorecard, just like the handoff checklist. If the market launched, the lessons learned document gets completed. Period.

> **Core principle:** The goal is not to assign blame. The goal is to build institutional memory. Every market teaches you something the playbook didn't cover. Your job is to capture that lesson so the next Market Launch Lead doesn't have to learn it the hard way. Write it like you're briefing your replacement — because you are.

---

## When to Complete

| Milestone | Deliverable | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **End of Soft Launch (Week 2-4)** | First draft — capture initial wins, surprises, and issues while they're fresh | Market Launch Lead |
| **At Handoff (Stabilization scorecard met)** | Final version — complete all sections, include metrics, get GM sign-off | Market Launch Lead + GM |
| **Anytime a significant issue or win occurs** | Quick-capture entry — don't wait for the formal review, log it immediately in the running document | Anyone on the launch team |

Do not wait until the formal review windows to start writing. The best lessons learned documents are built continuously — a running log that gets organized and polished at the milestone deadlines. If you wait until handoff to start writing, you'll forget the details that matter most.

## Section 3: What Didn't Go Well (Fix These)

Document the failures and near-misses. This is the most important section of the entire document. Be honest. Be specific. Include the impact — days delayed, dollars spent, customers affected. Vague entries like "licensing was slow" help no one. "State contractor license took 11 weeks instead of the planned 6 because we missed the notarization requirement on the initial application, delaying soft launch by 3 weeks" helps everyone.

**Categorize each item by severity:**

- **Critical** — Delayed the launch date
- **Major** — Required a workaround or significant extra effort
- **Minor** — Caused inconvenience but didn't impact the timeline

## Section 6: Playbook Feedback

The Expansion Playbook is a living document. Every Market Launch Lead who uses it has a responsibility to make it better for the next person. This section feeds directly into the quarterly playbook review cycle.

**What sections of the Expansion Playbook were most useful?**

**What sections were missing or incomplete?**

**What would you add or change?**

**Specific line items to update in the T-minus checklist (Appendix A-1)?**

Be specific. "Section 4.2 should include a note about state-specific bonding requirements for electrical contractors" is actionable. "The playbook needs improvement" is not.

## If You Only Remember 3 Things

1. **Complete this document for every market — no exceptions.** First draft at end of soft launch, final version at handoff. Don't wait until the end to start writing. Capture lessons in real time while the details are fresh.
2. **Section 4 (Surprises) is the most valuable section.** The things you didn't expect are the things the next Market Launch Lead won't expect either. Every surprise entry is a potential addition to the master checklist. This is how the playbook evolves.
3. **This is not bureaucracy — this is how the playbook gets better.** Every lessons learned document makes the next launch faster, cheaper, and less painful. Your 30 minutes of writing saves the next team 30 hours of problem-solving. That's the deal.
